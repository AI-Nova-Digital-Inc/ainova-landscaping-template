## Step 1 — Pick a High-Ticket Niche

### What Is a High-Ticket Niche

- A niche where the business owner charges high prices for their own services.
- The key insight: the higher the niche's own ticket price, the easier it is for them to justify spending money on a website.
- Niching down feels counterintuitive (limits your audience) but actually increases your income because you become specialized.

> "The more you niche down, the more money you can actually make."

### Low-Ticket vs High-Ticket Example

**Low-Ticket Example — Restaurant:**
- Restaurant sells sandwiches at $10 each, $5 profit per sandwich.
- To make $1,000 profit, the owner needs to sell 200 sandwiches.
- If you offer them a $1,000 website, they need 200 new clients just to break even on the website cost — a very hard justification.

**High-Ticket Example — Construction Business:**
- A construction business earns $10,000 profit from a single renovation job.
- If you offer them a $1,000 website, they only need one new client to recoup the cost.
- The purchase is immediately justifiable in their mind.

### How to Choose Your Niche

- Pick a niche you have personal experience with or connections to — you'll know the terminology and can speak their language on sales calls.
- Example: The presenter runs a landscaping business himself, so he sells to landscaping businesses and can speak their language directly.
- Example: If your friend's father owns a painting business, go into the painting niche — get insider knowledge from him.
- If you can't think of a niche, ask ChatGPT: *"Give me 10 high-ticket niches"* and pick one that resonates.
- The example niche used throughout this course: **construction businesses**.

## Step 2 — Client Acquisition

Three pillars to getting clients:
1. A high-ticket niche
2. Eyes on you (client acquisition methods)
3. A good offer

### Content Creation

- Best long-term client acquisition method.
- Post TikToks, Instagram Reels, YouTube videos consistently.
- The presenter currently gets all clients through content — no longer cold calls or cold DMs regularly.
- Requires being comfortable putting your face online.
- If you are shy or prefer privacy → use cold outreach instead.

### Cold Calling via Google Maps

**Process:**
1. Open Google and search your niche + location (e.g., *"Construction Calgary"*).
2. Click **Maps** in the search results.
3. Browse the listed businesses — each listing shows a phone number.
4. Create a spreadsheet to track companies and contact info.
5. Call each number and offer your web design services.
6. Before calling, check: do they have a website? Is it a good one?
7. Aim for at least **50 calls per day** — volume and practice are critical.

> "Numbers and just practicing is crucial for cold calls."

### Cold DMs via Instagram

**Process:**
1. Search your niche + location on Instagram (same logic as Google).
2. Follow the business account.
3. Like 3 of their posts.
4. Send an **initial message that is a question** — this gets you out of the "Requests" folder and into their main inbox.
   - Example opener: *"Hey, I just saw your profile and love your work — do you work in all of Calgary?"*
5. Once they reply (you're now in their main inbox), pitch them.

**Two pitch styles:**
- **Voice message** — works well if you have good tonality and are personable; they can hear your energy.
- **Text pitch** — example:
  > *"Awesome, I was just checking out your profile and saw your work, then I tried to click a link in your bio and noticed you didn't have a website. I thought it was a shame — you had such great work with nowhere to display it. I'm a web designer and thought I could help. I'll leave my number below — if you'd like a website, shoot me a text or call."*

- After sending, the ball is in their court.

### Cold Call Goals

When cold calling, have three objectives:

1. **Identify pain points** — e.g., for landscaping: *"We can't find consistent clients who don't cancel."*
2. **Identify their goals** — e.g., more clients, higher-quality leads, building trust through a professional website.
3. **Identify the decision maker** — always try to speak with the owner or someone who can actually approve a purchase. Receptionists whose only role is answering phones cannot make buying decisions — escalate.

## Step 3 — Creating an Amazing Offer

### Offer Structure

Example offer: **"The Ultimate Scaling Program for Construction Businesses Under $500,000/year"**

- Target: construction businesses under $500K/year that need more clients or higher-paying clients to reach $50K/month revenue.
- Present this offer on screen-share during sales calls so the client can see exactly what they're getting.

**Core deliverable — what the website includes:**
- Wireframe design
- Full development and publication
- Copywriting for the website

Framing: *"I'll make you a high-converting website using my proven structure, tested across multiple service-based businesses — one of my clients generated over $400,000 in their first year working with me."*

> Only use real statistics. If you don't have results yet, don't fabricate them — find another way to frame your credibility.

### Value Stacking

- List every component of what you're delivering so the client sees the full scope of work.
- When you reveal the price after the value stack, the client thinks: *"He's doing all of this for just that price? This is worth it."*
- Add a bonus beyond web design — something niche-specific (e.g., for landscaping clients, offer free lead generation strategies since the presenter has industry expertise).
- The bonus increases perceived value and makes the offer feel tailored to their exact business.

### Results-Driven vs Money-Driven Positioning

- Most web designers say: *"Work with me, I'll make you a website."* The only differentiator they offer is price — often lower than freelancers.
- They are **money-driven**: trying to take money from the client without offering real, specific value.
- Position yourself as **results-driven**: you genuinely want to grow their business.

> "I genuinely try to help my clients. The more your business grows, the happier you'll get — and it increases the chances of you referring your friends to me."

- On calls, tell clients explicitly: *"The chances of you referring friends to me increase if your business grows. My services will also be an additional case study for me."* This frames the relationship as mutually beneficial.

## Step 4 — Service Delivery and Hiring

### Learning to Build Websites Yourself

- Even if you plan to hire someone, learn the basics of building websites.
- Reason: clients often request small changes after delivery. If you can do them yourself, turnaround is faster and clients are happier — no need to wait for a third party.
- Recommended platform: **WordPress + Elementor** (popular, widely supported, easy to hand off to other developers if needed).
- The presenter has a full 2-hour website tutorial covering everything from hosting to domain to launch.

### Hiring on Fiverr

- Go to [Fiverr.com](https://www.fiverr.com) and search *"WordPress Elementor"*.
- Freelancers on Fiverr can build responsive WordPress websites for as low as **$129**.
- You sell the website to the client for $500–$3,000+, keep the difference.
- Margin example: sell for $1,000, pay Fiverr developer $129 → keep ~$871.
- Why WordPress + Elementor specifically: large talent pool on Fiverr means easy replacement if needed; avoid niche platforms with few developers.
- Contact freelancers via Fiverr's messaging system — get a quote, start the conversation.

## Tips and Tricks

### 1. Not Outreaching Enough

- When starting with no capital, time is your only asset — use all of it for outreach or content creation.
- The presenter sent Instagram DMs from his phone during slow periods at his day job.
- If you can make content → make content.
- If you can't → outreach relentlessly.
- Goal: get those first few clients as fast as possible through sheer volume.

### 2. Negotiating Your Prices

- Set a price and stick to it — never go lower, even by a small amount.
- Reason 1: if you undervalue yourself, you won't put in full effort → client gets a subpar result → bad review → damages your reputation.
- Reason 2: clients who negotiate are often the most difficult to work with. The presenter had a client who negotiated and it became a problematic relationship.

> "I much rather lose a client than go $2 lower."

- If a client tries to negotiate: *"I'm sorry, $500 is my fixed price."* That's it.

### 3. Setting Revision Limits

- Once the website is delivered, set a clear revision limit (e.g., **3 rounds of revisions**).
- Tell the client upfront: *"Once the site is built, you have 3 revision requests. Send all changes at once per round."*
- Without limits, some clients expect you to maintain and update the website indefinitely for free.
- This protects your time and sets professional expectations from the start.

### 4. Skipping Wireframes

- Always make a wireframe before building the website.
- A wireframe is a visual mockup/representation of what the final website will look like — takes about 1 hour to create.
- Process:
  1. Build the wireframe.
  2. Send it to the client.
  3. Client confirms: *"Yes, this is what I want."*
  4. Then build the actual website (takes ~3 days).
- Without a wireframe: you build for 3 days, client says they don't like it → 3 days wasted.
- With a wireframe: misalignment is caught in 1 hour, not 3 days.

### 5. Caring Too Much About the Sale

- On early sales calls, beginners are distracted by thoughts of the money — the $500, the commission.
- When your mind is on the sale, you stop listening to the client. Their words go in one ear and out the other.
- The fix: **focus entirely on helping the client**, not on closing.
- Ask questions: *"What is your goal for this business? What revenue do you want in the next 3 months? How can I help you get there?"*
- When you genuinely help, the sale closes naturally.

> "I genuinely try to help my clients, and if you genuinely try to help your client, the sale is just going to come — just trust me on this."

- This mindset comes with experience but is worth internalizing from day one.

## Generating Recurring Revenue

- Web design is traditionally a one-time payment model: client pays $1,000, project ends, no more income from that client.
- To grow a stable business, you need recurring monthly revenue.

**Solution: Hosting + Maintenance Retainer**

Offer clients two options:
1. **DIY hosting** — client sets up their own hosting (~$25/month).
2. **Managed hosting via you** — you handle hosting, maintenance, and a set number of monthly revisions.

**Pricing example:**
- Hosting cost to you: ~$25/month
- Charge client: ~$100/month
- Profit per client: ~$75/month (recurring)

**Compounding effect:**
- 10 clients on retainer → $750/month recurring
- 100 clients on retainer → $7,500/month recurring
- At scale, hire someone to handle revisions → becomes nearly passive income

What the retainer includes:
- Hosting setup and management
- Consistent website updates
- A set number of revisions per month (e.g., X revisions included)

> "At 100 clients, you're making $7,500 every single month coming into your account — and at that point you can start hiring people to do all those revisions for you."
