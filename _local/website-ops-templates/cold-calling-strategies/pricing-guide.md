## The Pricing Problem

Beginners get paralyzed between two extremes:

| Extreme | Example | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Too cheap | Fiverr sellers at $80/website | Not worth your time |
| Too expensive | Big agencies at $80,000 | Clients won't trust a beginner at that price |

Goal: find where to position yourself in between — based on market data, your actual costs, and your experience level.

---

## Golden Rule: Never Negotiate Price

> "The people that try to negotiate your prices are the exact clients that are going to be headaches and you don't want to work with them."

- Never drop your price when a client pushes back
- Clients who negotiate on price signal future problems
- Stand firm — if they don't see the value, they're not your client

## Step 1: Market Research

Find what people similar to you are charging right now.

**Who to look at:**
- Freelancers and small agencies **1–2 steps ahead of you** in the same niche and location
- Do NOT benchmark against large agencies charging $20,000+

**Where to research:**
- Google: `[niche] marketing agency` — e.g., `landscaping marketing agency`
  - Many agencies have a pricing page — use those as reference
- **Fiverr** — filter by your niche and location
- **Upwork** — same approach

**Example output from research:**
> If freelancers in America charge $1,000–$3,000 for a basic website, then $200 is too low and $10,000 is too high. Price somewhere in that band.

This gives you a realistic starting ballpark — not a final number.

## Step 2: Factor in Your Costs and Time

Market rate gives you a range. Now find a price that works **for you specifically** — not what a friend said, not what a YouTuber said.

### Costs to Account For

| Expense | Example Cost |
|---|---|
| Hosting platform (e.g., Cloudways) | ~$14/month for 5 websites |
| Alternative hosting (e.g., GoDaddy) | ~$20/website |
| Figma | Free / Paid |
| Webflow | Free / Paid |
| Other subscriptions | Varies |

### Time to Account For

Don't just count design time. Full project scope includes:
- Designing the website
- Maintaining the website post-launch
- Onboarding the client
- Ongoing client communication

**Sanity check example:**
> $500 profit on a project that takes 30 hours = $17/hour. Decide if that works for you.

Price must make sense **given your specific costs and hourly value** — no universal number works for everyone.

## Step 3: Choose a Pricing Model

Three models — pick based on your stage and niche.

### Model 1: Hourly Rate

- **Pros:** Simple to start with as a beginner
- **Cons:**
  - Caps your earnings
  - Penalizes you for working fast — the better and faster you get, the less you earn per project
  - Not recommended long-term

### Model 2: Project-Based Pricing *(Recommended)*

- Fixed price per deliverable — e.g., **$2,000 for a 5-page website**
- Most common model for beginners and high-scale agencies
- Creator's current model

**Why it works:**
> "One day I work with a landscaping company. It takes me X hours. I charge $2,000. Next landscaping client — I already know what works. Takes me half the time. Still make $2,000."

Speed improvements directly increase your effective hourly rate without changing what you charge.

### Model 3: Value-Based Pricing

- Charge a **percentage of results** delivered — common with e-commerce clients
- Example: 10% of $100,000/month profit = **$10,000/month**
- Only use if you can confidently guarantee results
- Creator has not personally used this model — best for experienced agencies with proven ROI track record

## Step 4: Create Flexible Packages

Never present a single price — always offer tiered packages. Avoids all-or-nothing situations.

### Example Package Structure

| Package | Includes | Price |
|---|---|---|
| **Package A** | Homepage only | $2,000 |
| **Package B** | Homepage + Service Page + Basic SEO | $3,000 |
| **Package C** | Full site + additional services | $3,500+ |

### Presentation Strategy

1. **Always present the most expensive package first**
2. Work downward based on client's budget
3. This anchors them to the higher number — smaller packages feel like a deal

## Step 5: Raise Prices Over Time

Price increases are earned through:
- Better/faster process
- Stronger portfolio
- Proven results
- More confidence on sales calls

> "Clients are not only paying for the design — they're paying for the confidence, the speed, and the results you bring based on your experience."

### When to Raise Prices

**Signal to raise:** Closing too many clients — if you hop on 5 calls and close all 5, your price is too low. Raise it.

**Signal to hold:** Too many clients saying you're too expensive. Slow down the increases.

**Target balance:** Not closing everyone, but not getting too many "too expensive" objections either. Find the middle where there's healthy friction.

## Summary

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Research what freelancers 1–2 steps ahead charge (Google, Fiverr, Upwork) |
| 2 | Factor in your real costs + time — make sure the number works for you |

| 4 | Offer 3 tiered packages — present most expensive first |
| 5 | Raise prices when you close too many clients too easily |

**Ballpark starting range (US market):** $1,000–$3,000 per website

**Never negotiate.** Clients who haggle are the worst clients to work with.
